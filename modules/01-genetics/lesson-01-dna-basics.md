## 1. What is DNA?

DNA stands for **deoxyribonucleic acid**. It is a molecule that carries information passed from parents to children.

Most DNA in a human cell is inside a part of the cell called the nucleus. A smaller amount is found in another part of the cell called the mitochondria.

You can think of DNA as a very long set of instructions written with four letters:

| Letter | Name |
|---|---|
| A | Adenine |
| T | Thymine |
| C | Cytosine |
| G | Guanine |

DNA usually has two strands that fit together. The letters pair in a specific way:

- A pairs with T.
- C pairs with G.

For example:

```text
First strand:  A T G C C A T G
Second strand: T A C G G T A C
```

The order of these letters is called a DNA sequence.

## 2. DNA, genes, and chromosomes

These three words are related, but they do not mean the same thing.

- **DNA** is the molecule that carries genetic information.
- **A gene** is a section of DNA that has a role in how the body works.
- **A chromosome** is a long DNA molecule organized with proteins.

Most human body cells have 46 chromosomes. They are arranged in 23 pairs. Usually, one chromosome in each pair comes from the biological mother and the other comes from the biological father.

## 3. How does a child inherit DNA?

When a child is conceived, the egg and sperm each contribute genetic material.

For most of the DNA markers we will study in this course, a child receives one version from the biological mother and one version from the biological father.

Different versions of the same marker are called **alleles**. We will explain this word, along with another word called **locus**, in the next lesson.

## 4. Why does this matter in a paternity test?

A paternity test looks at selected places in a person's DNA. Scientists compare the child's results with the results from the mother and the alleged father.

The child's DNA should make sense based on what could have been inherited from each biological parent.

Here is a made-up example:

| Person | Results at one DNA marker |
|---|---|
| Mother | 10, 12 |
| Alleged father | 14, 16 |
| Child | 12, 14 |

The child could have inherited 12 from the mother and 14 from the alleged father. So these results do not rule out the alleged father at this one marker.

But one marker is not enough to establish paternity. A real test examines many markers and uses calculations to assess the results.
